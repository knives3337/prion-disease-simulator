How it works

A shape, not a germ

Prions contain no DNA or RNA. A prion is the protein PrP folded wrong: its spiral α-helices flatten into β-sheets. Those sheets stick together and act as a template, so every healthy PrP that touches them gets refolded the same bad way.

Why the brain gets spongy

Misfolded PrP piles up into plaques and fibrils, and neurons die. The dead cells leave tiny holes, which is why the diseases are called spongiform encephalopathies.

Cooking doesn't help

Prions survive boiling, frying, freezing, radiation and most disinfectants. That's why the UK and EU ban the highest-risk tissues (brain, spinal cord) from the food chain.

Today it's very rare

After the BSE crisis, feed bans and tissue controls ended the epidemic. The UK recorded 178 vCJD deaths, peaking in 2000. Most CJD today is sporadic, about 1–2 cases per million people a year, and has nothing to do with diet.
